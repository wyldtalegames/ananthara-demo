Long before you reach the doors of the Great Hall, this part of Ithar’kael sounds different from the archive or the quieter living quarters. Footsteps echo over stone. Voices come and go. Something is being carried, and elsewhere final instructions are exchanged.
Then the doors open before you.
The Great Hall earns its name. Its vault rises far above you, and even the people moving beneath it seem smaller than usual for a moment.

Yet your gaze does not remain on them for long.
At the far end of the hall stands the portal.
An immense stone frame rises from the floor. At present, nothing lies within its arch but a still, dark surface, yet fine lines already travel over the stone’s edge and disappear again, as though something within it merely waits to be awakened.
You have seen this portal often enough.
Envoys leaving Ithar’kael. Others returning after long journeys. Luggage, maps, reports and sometimes things that found their way into the archives only hours later.
Today, you are not here to watch someone else depart.

Corvus waits before the portal.
He stands half-turned away from you, studying something upon a narrow lectern beside it. Only when your steps draw closer does he look up. “There you are.” His gaze moves briefly past you through the hall, then returns. “I hope you made use of your free time.”

He does not ask whether you visited the archive, sat in the courtyard or withdrew to your room. Corvus was rarely someone who demanded an answer when it was none of his concern.

Instead, he rests one hand upon the documents before him.
“Before you leave, there is something else we should discuss.”

For a moment you think again of the book in the final examination chamber. Of the light on its pages. Of the questions it answered.

Corvus seems to know where your thoughts have gone.

“The replica you encountered during your examination was not a randomly chosen artefact,” he says. “It was a copy of the Chronicles.”

The Memory of Creation.

“What you saw was limited. It could work only with what you brought to it—your knowledge, your memories and the possibilities already within you.”

“The original has no such limitation.”

He opens a record. “According to Ithar’kael’s records, the Chronicles were removed from the place where they were meant to be preserved. The place whose image you saw during your examination.”

His finger rests on one line.

“Stolen.”

“Since then, there have been reports of their whereabouts. Fragments. Rumours. Clues that partly contradict one another. Enough to know the search is not over. Not enough to place a point on a map.”

“Ithar’kael intends to find the Chronicles and return them safely to the place where they belong.”

Then he looks directly at you.

“You will follow one of those leads.”

“Your examination did not determine whether you know every answer. It showed that you know how to search. Compare reports. Test claims. Distinguish possibility from certainty.” The corner of his mouth shifts almost imperceptibly. “You should feel almost at home by now.”

“Discover what is true in the reports concerning the Chronicles. Follow the traces. And if you find the original, bring it back.”

Corvus leaves the personal question there. No promise that you will find the answer. No attempt to tell you what it should mean in the end.

Then he turns toward the portal.

“Before you leave, you should know how to return. And how to get out at all.”

Corvus gestures toward the signs in the stone arch.

Only now, from close by, do you see how many there truly are. Some lie almost hidden among finer lines and geometric patterns. Others are larger and more clearly worked.

The tree. The sun. The snow crystal.

“Each of these signs carries a connection to a place,” Corvus explains. “But a symbol alone opens no path.”

His gaze falls to your hand.

“You must establish the connection yourself.”

For a moment, you think of the final examination chamber. Of the exposed channel. Of how your own Thal’ithara first had to flow into the existing pattern before the energy could be carried onward.

“You have already applied the principle today,” Corvus says. He briefly rests his fingertips against the portal’s outer edge without touching any of the signs. “Choose your destination. Touch its sign. Let your Thal’ithara flow into it and hold the connection. Once it is stable, the portal will accept it and open the passage.”

“There are more paths than these three. But for your first departure, these are the ones open to you.” Then Corvus steps back from the portal. “From this point on, Ithar’kael will not place every question before you.” His hand rests on the lectern’s edge for a moment. “Some you will have to recognise for yourself.”

Then he looks at you once more.

“The Chronicles are your assignment. But Ananthara is more than the objective you are given. You will come to know places and cultures, follow ancient myths, and encounter relics and stories no one sent you to find. Which of these trails you pursue is up to you.” He steps aside. “When you are ready, choose your first path.”

You remain before the portal. Its interior is still dark. Only the fine lines in the stone frame glow faintly, waiting for a destination to be chosen. You have seen this portal many times. Today it seems different. Perhaps because, for the first time, you know that the next person to pass through it will be you.

Suddenly everything feels more tangible. The examination is over. The years of learning lie behind you. The reports you studied in the archive. The conversations in the courtyard. The questions Corvus asked again and again. All the times you watched other envoys leave Ithar’kael.

Until now, the world beyond these halls was something for which you prepared. Now it waits on the other side of a single step. You came to Ithar’kael because you sought answers. Not just any truth. Yours.
